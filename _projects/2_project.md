In the early 2000's, biologists in British Columbia's Lower Fraser Valley observed an unexpected (but perhaps not entirely unfamiliar) floral visitor: <em>Bombus impatiens</em>, the Common Eastern Bumblebee (CITE RATTI).

The name and image of <em>Bombus impatiens</em> will be familiar to any pollination ecologist who has set foot in eastern North America or dipped a toe into the bumblebee experimental ecology literature . Much like <em>B. terrestris</em> in Europe, <em>B. impatiens</em> is a North American work-horse for lab- and field-based studies that require rearing colonies in captivity, an approach made possible by concerted efforts in the 1990s to establish commercial stocks of several North American bumblebee species for pollination service provisioning.  Following failed domestication attempts in <em>B. occidentalis</em>, BC's government green-lit the importation of <em>B. impatiens</em> colonies to pollinate greenhouse tomatoes, bell peppers and cucumbers.

In a turn of events which should surprise no one, the ever-resourceful <em>B. impatiens</em> flew the coop and quickly established itself in the farmlands of southwestern British Columbia. In 2022, when my field work in this region began, nearly 40% of all the bumblebee workers we observed between May and August were <em>B. impatiens</em>, highlighting the remarkable speed with which this species has come to dominate pollinator communities.
